# Round 12: auditing what the broken instrument had already told me

Round 11 found that the bench replanned the planner's own output. That bench produced
rounds 9 and 10, so before building anything else on those conclusions they were re-run
with a single pass.

## 1. Three conclusions audited, two unchanged, one stronger, one overturned

| what round 10 concluded | one pass | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| without the seen-floor fallback there are **no rooms** | 0 of 3, same | **survives** |
| the wall cleanup (`tidy`) changes **nothing at all** | numbers identical to the baseline | **survives** |
| clipping the interior to the real floor removes half of it and fixes the inflated room | removes **20.8 of 41.7 m²**, mean per-room error **33 %** against a baseline of 176 % | **survives, and now passes the 40 % threshold it missed** |
| **no stage owns the error** (the best switch was worth 11 points) | switching off the Manhattan rounding takes the flat from 2 rooms and 176 % to **3 rooms and 76 %** | **overturned on this scene** |

The last one is what an audit is for. On the two-pass cloud the Manhattan rotation was
already applied, so switching it off in the second pass changed almost nothing; in one pass
it is a hundred points on the flat.

**And then the bench said no anyway.** Over all seven scenes, `manhattan=False`:

| | mean area error | rooms matched | TUM |
|---|---|---|---|
| today | **17 %** | 6 of 8 | 3 walls, 1 room, +0 % |
| without the rounding | 35 % | 6 of 8 | **32 walls, 3 rooms, −28 %** |

One scene gains three rooms, six scenes get worse, and the published TUM example is
destroyed. The default does not move, and the round's one planner change stays unspent.
This is exactly the case the one-change-per-round rule exists for: a hundred-point win on
the scene you happen to be looking at.

## 2. Extent and density are two questions, and only one has a number

The previous sweep took *prefixes* of the walk, so every row walked a different distance.
That measures **extent**, and its answer is worth writing down on its own: rooms appear as
the walk reaches them, 1, 2, 1, 2, 3 of three, and not before.

**Density** needs the whole walk at every row, with only the spacing changing. The
prediction written before running was that the room count would be 2 or 3 from 20 views up
and that the error would flatten between 58 and 116. Half right:

| views | walked | rooms found | fused | area | mean per-room \|e\| | floor seen |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 18 m | 1 of 3 | 1 | 59.2 m² (+14 %) | 223 % | 25 % |
| 40 | 23 m | **3 of 3** | 0 | 62.4 m² (+21 %) | **85 %** | 37 % |
| 60 | 22 m | 2 of 3 | 1 | 76.5 m² (+48 %) | 186 % | 42 % |
| 80 | 23 m | **3 of 3** | 0 | 63.4 m² (+22 %) | **85 %** | 45 % |
| 116 | 24 m | **3 of 3** | 0 | 63.6 m² (+23 %) | **85 %** | 46 % |

It flattens from 40 views, at exactly 85 %, and 20 spread that thin is not enough. The
prediction that every row would find 2 or 3 rooms was wrong: 20 views over 18 m still fuses.

## 3. The error takes one of two values, not a range

Across the ten runs of this round and the last, the mean per-room error is not a
continuum. Whenever the three rooms come out separate it is **85 %**; whenever two of them
fuse into one blob it is **175-223 %**. Nothing lands in between. The 60-view spike is not
a density effect, it is the decomposition flipping mode on a slightly different set of
frames.

So the planner has a bimodal failure, and the `fused` column is the one that says which
mode a run is in. A mean error quoted without it is unreadable.

## 4. What the capture guide now says

Both language versions gained the measured statement: **walk into every room, and past
roughly 40 frames spread over the house, more footage of the same path adds nothing.** With
the numbers, including the one that keeps the advice honest: twenty-four metres of walking
in a 52 m² flat still leaves floor points on only 46 % of its floor, so what matters is
where the camera points, not how long it runs.
