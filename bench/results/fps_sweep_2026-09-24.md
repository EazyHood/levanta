# More frames made it worse: the fps sweep, and what it says about the default

The question was what the default sampling should be. `levanta video` takes 1 frame per
second and a phone films at 30 or 60, so the default throws away 97-98 % of what the person
recorded, and a warning shipped on 2026-09-06 told users with dense videos to run again with
`--fps 2`. This sweep was meant to measure the knee.

Subject: the two ARKitScenes rooms whose `.mov` is on disk, both 60 fps with LiDAR truth,
the whole pipeline at 1, 2, 4 and 8 fps (`bench/fps_sweep.py`). Truth here is the ARKitScenes
bench's own floor definition (17.5 and 4.9 m²).

## The prediction, written before the first run

> Area error improves clearly from 1 to 2 fps, little from 2 to 4, and not at all from 4 to
> 8, because past a few frames per m² the extra views land on surfaces already covered. The
> knee is expected near 2-4 fps. The output is the rule: sample until the plan reaches the
> minimum density, under a time ceiling, without asking.

**Wrong in direction, not in size.** Nothing saturated. On the larger room every honest
number got worse with every step.

## What happened

| fps | scene | frames | scale | area, scale corrected | floor IoU | rooms | camera drift |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 41069021 (17.5 m²) | 184 | 1.03 | −21 % | **0.65** | 1 | **0.47 m** |
| 2 | 41069021 | 361 | 0.87 | −38 % | 0.55 | 3 | 0.65 m |
| 4 | 41069021 | 715 | 0.45 | −83 % | 0.16 | 1 | 1.02 m |
| 8 | 41069021 | 1 332 | 0.33 | −83 % | 0.16 | 1 | 0.96 m |
| 1 | 42897526 (4.9 m²) | 46 | 1.00 | −19 % | 0.43 | 1 | 0.49 m |
| 2 | 42897526 | 92 | 1.15 | +24 % | 0.52 | 2 | 0.35 m |
| 4 | 42897526 | 183 | 1.19 | +38 % | **0.61** | 2 | **0.27 m** |
| 8 | 42897526 | 341 | 1.29 | −19 % | 0.32 | 1 | 0.35 m |

On the larger room, 1 fps is best on every column. On the small one, 4 fps has the best
IoU and the least drift, and invents a second room in a one-room scene while its scale is
off by 19 %.

**And the column a user actually sees lies again.** Uncorrected area on the larger room reads
−25, −18, −15 and +59 %: it looks like it improves from 1 to 4 fps while the reconstruction
comes apart. At 4 fps the reconstruction is 2.2 times too large and its shape is 83 % short,
and the two cancel to −15 %. That is the fourth time in this project a respectable number has
been two errors of opposite sign, and it is now a rule of every bench report rather than a
sentence here: an area error is printed with its shape and its scale, or not at all
(`bench/area_report.py`).

## Why: the number of chunks, not the number of frames

MapAnything runs in chunks of 24 views with 4 shared, and each chunk's scale is aligned to the
previous one. At a fixed chunk size, more frames per second means shorter chunks and more of
them, and the scale is composed from one to the next.

| fps | chunks, larger room | video per chunk | scale error | chunks, small room | scale error |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 9 | 20.9 s | 3 % | 3 | 0 % |
| 2 | 18 | 10.5 s | 15 % | 5 | 15 % |
| 4 | 36 | 5.2 s | 123 % | 9 | 19 % |
| 8 | 67 | 2.8 s | 206 % | 17 | 30 % |

Scale error here is the size of the correction the trajectory alignment has to apply, as a
magnitude: the reconstruction drifts too big in one room and too small in the other, so a
correlation with sign reads −0.89 on the small room and hides that the error grows in both.
It grows at **every step in both rooms**. On the larger room at 8 fps one chunk needed a scale
of 2.01 to meet its neighbour, and the product of the 66 chunk-to-chunk scales is 55 854.

Two rooms and four points each is thin, and it is enough to say the proposed rule was wrong:
**sampling to a density makes it worse while the chunk size stays fixed.**

**The defect is the chain, not the chunk length** (the supervisor's reading, and the right
one). Longer chunks are fewer links in the same chain: they would attenuate the error, not
remove it. As long as each chunk's scale is inherited from the one before, a long enough
video breaks it, and a whole house in one take is longer than these two rooms. So the design
question is whether the scale can be fixed **once** for the whole walk, not how long a chunk
should be. If it cannot, levanta has a ceiling on video length and has to say so before
anyone records forty minutes.

## The chain drifts one way, it does not wander

A chain of unbiased noise would have about half its links above 1 and half below, and its
product would wander around 1. These do not:

| run | links | links that shrink the next chunk | mean change per link | product |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 fps, larger room | 8 | 5 | −5.2 % | 0.66 |
| 2 fps, larger room | 17 | 13 | −7.5 % | 0.27 |
| 4 fps, larger room | 35 | **27** | **−10.6 %** | **0.020** |
| 8 fps, larger room | 66 | 18 | +18.1 % | 55 854 |
| 1 fps, small room | 2 | 2 | −8.7 % | 0.83 |
| 2 fps, small room | 4 | 2 | −2.8 % | 0.90 |
| 4 fps, small room | 8 | 4 | −0.9 % | 0.93 |
| 8 fps, small room | 16 | 13 | −6.9 % | 0.32 |

The mean change per link is negative in seven of eight runs, and 27 of 35 links shrinking is
not a coin (under 1 in 1 000 by chance). A bias compounds geometrically, which is why the
error grows at every step instead of spreading out: 0.894 to the 35th power is the 0.020 in
the table. The estimator itself is a median of depth ratios, which has no bias of its own. What it
compares is what the network says about the **same four frames** at the end of one chunk and
at the start of the next, and more often than not it puts them nearer at the end. Those four
frames then go into the next chunk **with their poses already scaled by the chain**, so the
scale is inherited twice: once through the ratio, once through the poses. Which end is wrong
cannot be told from the ratio alone. The one run that drifts the other way is the 8 fps run
on the larger room. The 8 fps run on the small room drifts like the rest, so the sign is not
simply the frame rate, and why that one reversed is not known.

## What levanta says about it now

- **The case nothing flagged is flagged.** The 4 fps run drew the plan 2.2 times too big with
  a shape 83 % short, and its sheet carried no warning: every link sat inside [0.5, 2] and
  their spread was 1.79, under the 2.5 the mirror check allows. The product of the links
  showed it. The two parts of that walk furthest apart in size were 52 times apart, against
  1.15 to 4.64 on every walk that did not break. Plans now carry a warning and the stamp
  *NOT RECONSTRUCTIBLE · scale lost along the walk* above 10 times, in that
  order-of-magnitude gap. On the eight plans of this sweep it fires on exactly the two broken
  ones.
- **`levanta check` counts the chunks before any GPU time** and warns past 9, the longest
  chain measured against a real floor at the default settings (about three minutes at 1 fps).
  On the one real tour in the repository it prints 8 chunks at 1 fps and a warning at 2 fps.
- **The capture guide says it in both languages:** past three minutes in one take is past
  anything measured, and nothing longer has been measured.
- **No area error in any bench report stands alone any more.** Each one prints its raw figure
  with its shape and its scale, and a test fails when a bench prints it any other way. On its
  first run it found a fifth case of two opposite errors: ARKitScenes 45260905 reads −28 %,
  which is a plan 1.31 times too small with a shape 22 % too big.

## What is still open: can the scale be fixed once

One GPU pass per scene answers it without a new walk: reconstruct every chunk on its own, with
no poses fed in, keep the raw output, and recompose it on the CPU three ways against the same
truth:

1. chained, as today;
2. each chunk at the network's own metric scale, aligned only in rotation and translation;
3. a joint fit that keeps every chunk near its own scale while agreeing with its neighbours.

The test is the larger room at 1 fps (9 chunks) and at 4 fps (36). If one of them holds the
scale across 36 chunks, the chain goes. If none does, the ceiling is real, and the warning at 9
chunks is its first statement.

## What changes because of this

- **The default stays at 1 fps.** Of the four tested it has the smallest scale error on both
  rooms, and on the larger one it is best on every column.
- **The warning that says "run it again with `--fps 2`" was promising something the data
  contradicts.** On the larger room `--fps 2` lost ten points of IoU, invented two rooms and
  took the scale 13 % off. The message now reports the frames that were left unused, keeps not
  blaming the person who filmed, and no longer promises that more of them would help.
- **The experiment cost more than it should have.** See the watcher section below.

## The watcher, which ran the same half hour of GPU at every boot for eleven days

1, 2 and 4 fps ran on 2026-09-12 and verified. 8 fps started at 16:49 that day and the log
never recorded its end. From then on the task, triggered at every logon, found no record for
8 fps and ran it again: nine times between the 14th and the 23rd, each start followed by
nothing. On the 23rd the child finished at 11:11 with both scenes ok, and the watcher had
already died: the task's last result is `0xC000013A`, a console-control termination, with no
farewell line, so it was not a Python exception. Who ended it is not determinable here, since
the task history on this machine is off. What was ruled out, from the machine's own records:

- a shutdown, logoff or sleep: the system log has the boot at 10:34:58 and nothing of the kind
  until 18:30;
- a crash: no Application Error for Python that day;
- the task's own limits: no time limit, and not stopped on battery;
- the console blocker the machine runs against pop-ups: it hides windows, never closes them,
  and its log has no action between its start at 10:36 and 13:12;
- the other schedulers on the machine: they kill only their own processes by PID, with
  `taskkill /F`, which leaves exit code 1, not this one.

And one pattern that does not depend on the killer: in none of the nine runs did the watcher
write a line after "running fps 8". With the old design, that is exactly what a watcher that
dies before its child looks like.

What made it expensive was the design, not the killer:

- completion was recorded only by the watcher, after the child returned, so a watcher that
  dies turns a finished run into a "never launched" one, forever;
- a child outlived its watcher and kept the card with nobody supervising it;
- the game check ran only between runs, and 8 fps is a 35-minute run.

The task was disabled on 2026-09-23 before anything else. The fixes are in `bench/when_idle.py`
and `bench/fps_sweep.py`: a record written before launch and read back, a run counted as
finished from its own results when the watcher's record is missing (and labelled as such), the
child placed in a job object so it dies with its watcher, and the game check running every 30
seconds during a run, stopping the child if a game starts.
